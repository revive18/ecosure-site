What Is Black Mold — and Is It Dangerous?

Short answer: "Black mold" usually means Stachybotrys chartarum, a dark mold that grows on damp drywall and wood. Any indoor mold should be taken seriously — some people react with allergy and respiratory symptoms — but you can't confirm black mold by color alone. Only a lab test identifies the species, and the fix is the same either way: find the moisture, confirm the extent, and remove it safely.

What "black mold" actually is

The term usually points to Stachybotrys chartarum, a greenish-black mold that thrives on cellulose-rich materials — drywall, wood, cardboard — that have stayed wet for a while. It's real, but it's also surrounded by a lot of hype. Many other completely common molds are also dark, so a black patch is not automatically Stachybotrys.

Is it dangerous?

Here's the balanced view: any active mold growth indoors should be addressed, and people with allergies, asthma, or weakened immune systems can be more sensitive to mold exposure, with symptoms like congestion, coughing, irritated eyes, or worsened asthma. At the same time, the sensational claims you sometimes read online outrun the science. The practical takeaway doesn't change with the color: locate the water source, confirm how far the growth extends, and have it removed properly.

You can't diagnose it by color

This is the key point most homeowners miss. Appearance is unreliable — you cannot tell Stachybotrys from a dozen harmless dark molds just by looking. The only way to know what you actually have is a surface sample (swab or tape-lift) analyzed by an accredited lab, ideally alongside an air test to see whether it's spreading beyond what's visible.

What to do if you find dark mold

  • Don't disturb or scrub it — that can spread spores. Limit airflow to the area.
  • Fix the moisture source (leak, humidity) — mold can't grow without it.
  • Get it tested to confirm the species and how far it extends before any cleanup.
