Why Your Remediation Company Shouldn't Test Their Own Work

Many remediation contractors in Los Angeles offer to perform clearance testing at the end of the job, often bundled into the price. The convenience is real. The problem is equally real: the company being tested is the company doing the testing.

No contractor sets out to pass themselves fraudulently. But a failed clearance means returning to the site, additional labor, and an uncomfortable conversation with the client — and the same person is deciding whether that happens. This is precisely the scenario independent verification exists to solve.

Eco Sure Testing performs no remediation of any kind. We have never worked on your property, we have no relationship with your contractor, and our fee does not change based on whether the area passes.

What Clearance Testing Involves

  • Visual inspection of the remediated area for remaining growth, debris, or staining
  • Verification that containment was maintained and the work area was properly cleaned
  • Moisture readings to confirm affected materials have dried to normal levels
  • Outdoor control air sample collected the same day
  • Indoor air samples inside the remediated area
  • Comparison against the outdoor baseline and, where available, pre-remediation data
  • Written pass or fail determination with full lab documentation

Timing Matters

Clearance testing should happen after remediation and drying are complete, but before containment barriers come down and before any reconstruction begins. Once new drywall goes up, a failed area becomes enormously more expensive to correct — and testing a rebuilt space cannot tell you what was behind the wall.

If your contractor is pushing to close things up before an independent test, that timing is worth questioning.

When Clearance Testing Is Required

  • Insurance claims requiring third-party verification
  • Real estate transactions with a remediation disclosure
  • Landlord–tenant habitability disputes
  • Remediation contracts specifying independent clearance
  • Litigation or documented liability exposure
  • Any large-scale remediation you paid significant money for

Pass, Fail, and What Comes Next

A pass means the remediated area shows no visible growth, materials have dried to acceptable moisture levels, and indoor spore counts and species profile are consistent with the outdoor control. You have documentation that the work was completed properly — useful for insurance, for a future sale, and for your own peace of mind.

A fail means conditions have not returned to normal. That does not automatically mean the contractor did poor work; it can also mean the underlying moisture source was never resolved, or that containment failed and spores spread during the job. The report documents what was found and where, so your contractor knows exactly what to address. The area can then be retested.

Either way, the finding comes from a party who does not benefit from the result.

Common Questions

What is post-remediation clearance testing?

It is independent verification that a completed mold remediation actually returned the space to normal fungal conditions. It combines a visual inspection, moisture readings, and air sampling compared against an outdoor control.

Can my remediation company do its own clearance test?

Some offer it, but it means the contractor is grading their own work. Independent clearance testing is performed by a party with no financial interest in the outcome, which is why insurers, attorneys, and many contracts require it.

When should clearance testing be performed?

After remediation work and drying are complete but before containment barriers are removed and before any rebuild begins. Testing after the walls are closed up defeats the purpose.

What happens if the clearance test fails?

A failed clearance means conditions have not returned to normal. The report documents what was found so the remediation contractor can address it, after which the area can be retested.
